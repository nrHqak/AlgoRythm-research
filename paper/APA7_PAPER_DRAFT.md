## Abstract

Novice programmers spend a disproportionate share of debugging time failing to localize logical faults, and automated fault-localization techniques built for mature, professional software degrade sharply when applied to novice code. Large language models (LLMs) have recently been shown to outperform classical spectrum- and mutation-based localization on novice programs, but their accuracy still declines with task difficulty, and no published method conditions localization on the *algorithmic pattern* (e.g., two pointers, dynamic programming) that structures a novice's intended solution. This pre-registered calibration pilot tests whether supplying an algorithm-pattern-specific structural prior improves LLM-based fault localization relative to a matched, pattern-agnostic ("generic") debugging prior of equal format, length, and instructional tone — the comparison that isolates the causal contribution of pattern specificity from the confound of simply providing more structured guidance. Thirty single-line-fault Python programs were drawn by a fully specified, seeded sampling pipeline from ConDefects-Python, spanning four algorithmic-pattern classes with human-assigned pattern labels. Each program is scored under three prompt conditions (no prior, matched generic prior, pattern-specific prior) using one frozen LLM configuration, with the pre-registered primary analysis an exact McNemar test comparing Top-1 localization accuracy between the pattern-prior and generic-prior conditions at the level of the program. As of this writing, every attempted execution of the frozen protocol has terminated in a technical VOID caused by output-token exhaustion before any of the 600 planned scientific calls could be fully collected, and no Top-*K*, EXAM, or discordance estimate exists. *[RESULT PENDING — TO BE INSERTED AFTER A VALID PILOT RUN COMPLETES.]* This report documents the frozen protocol, dataset, and pre-registered analysis plan in full ahead of data collection, so that the eventual result — whatever its direction — can be reported against commitments made before it was known.

## Introduction

### The Difficulty of Logical Debugging in Novice Programs

Programming novices lose a large share of their working time not to syntax errors, which compilers report directly, but to *logical* faults — code that runs and produces output but implements the wrong behavior. Du Boulay's (1986) classic five-area account of learning difficulties places "pragmatics" — planning, testing, and debugging — as a distinct and persistently under-taught competency, separate from mastering the language's notation or its execution model. Multi-institutional studies of how novices actually debug converge on the same picture: Fitzgerald et al. (2008) characterize novice debugging as *flailing* — unplanned trial-and-error with weak hypothesis generation — and McCauley et al.'s (2008) review of three decades of debugging-education research concludes that debugging is rarely taught explicitly and that novice strategies are poorly matched to the fault types they actually encounter. Underlying this behavioral evidence is a cognitive account of *why* novice faults are hard to find: Spohrer and Soloway (1986) showed, against the folk wisdom of the time, that most observed novice errors are not superficial misunderstandings of language syntax ("symbolic bugs") but failures at the level of *plan composition* — the point where two or more programming plans are integrated, which is exactly where an automated tool with no notion of the intended plan has the least information to work with.

### Limitations of Syntax- and Pattern-Agnostic Debugging

Classical automated fault-localization (FL) techniques were developed and validated on mature, professional software, and they transfer poorly to novice code. Spectrum-based fault localization (SBFL) ranks program elements by how often they are exercised by failing versus passing tests (Jones et al., 2002; surveyed comprehensively in Wong et al., 2016), but Araujo et al. (2016) report that roughly 40% of novice programs violate SBFL's basic precondition of having both passing and failing tests, and even where the precondition holds, short novice programs produce many suspiciousness ties that erode Top-*K* accuracy. Mutation-based fault localization (MBFL) is more robust in principle, but Yang et al.'s (2025) direct empirical comparison on ConDefects — the dataset this study also uses — found that even the best MBFL configuration reaches only Top-5 ≈ 61% on Python novice programs, at a mutation-testing cost of roughly 87 machine-hours for 150 programs. LLM-based localizers are a substantial improvement: Xu et al. (2025), evaluating thirteen models including OpenAI's o3 and DeepSeek-R1 across three novice-fault benchmarks, found that reasoning LLMs beat SBFL and MBFL on almost every dataset and metric, and MacNeil et al. (2024) found that GPT-4 significantly outperforms a sample of 964 introductory students at logic-error *detection*. But both properties that make classical FL brittle for novices — the absence of any notion of the programmer's intended plan, and sensitivity to how "clean" the fault is — persist in the LLM setting: Xu et al. (2025) report that every LLM's accuracy degrades as task difficulty rises, and none of the thirteen models they evaluate receives any structural signal about which algorithmic technique the program is attempting. Pattern-agnostic localization, LLM-based or classical, treats a two-pointer program and a dynamic-programming program identically at the prompt level; nothing in the pipeline exploits the fact that different plans fail in different, well-documented ways.

### Code Syntax Versus Algorithmic Intent

The theoretical case for exploiting intent rests on program-comprehension research. Brooks (1983) argues that expert comprehension proceeds by generating and verifying hypotheses about a program's goals, cued by *beacons* — distinctive code features that signal a familiar idiom. Pennington (1987) and von Mayrhauser and Vans (1995) show that experienced readers build not only a text-level control-flow representation of code but a *situation model* of what the program is trying to accomplish, and that novices reliably stall at the former without reaching the latter. An algorithmic pattern — "this is a two-pointer solution," "this is a dynamic-programming table fill" — is a situation-model fact at a higher level of abstraction than any single beacon, and it licenses a specific, well-documented set of hypotheses about where an implementation of that pattern is likely to go wrong (e.g., pointer-shift direction and stopping conditions for two pointers; base-case value and transition completeness for dynamic programming, the latter independently supported by Baron and Feitelson's 2024 finding that even experienced programmers' recursion difficulties concentrate on base-case recognition). Code syntax alone — the token stream a pattern-agnostic localizer or classical FL formula actually sees — does not encode this; two syntactically similar nested loops can be a brute-force enumeration or a dynamic-programming fill, and the plausible fault sites differ sharply between them.

### Structural Priors as a Solution: From Notional Machines to Pattern-Aware Localization

The idea of supplying an explicit, higher-level model of what a program is doing, in order to compensate for a reasoner's incomplete internal model, has a direct intellectual lineage. The notional-machine literature (du Boulay et al., 1981; Sorva, 2013; Fincher et al., 2020) argues that novices' mental models of program execution are systematically incomplete, and that making the intended machine explicit — as a designed, teachable artifact — helps. Johnson's (1990) PROUST system operationalized a closely related idea for automated diagnosis three decades before large language models existed: PROUST diagnosed novice Pascal bugs by comparing the student's code against a hand-built library of expected *programming plans* for a specific assignment, treating deviations from the expected plan as evidence of the underlying bug. PROUST's library was hand-authored per assignment and never evaluated with a quantitative Top-*K* localization metric against baselines; this study asks the modern version of PROUST's question — does telling a fault localizer which plan the programmer intended help it find the bug faster — using a *classified, transferable, pattern-level* prior rather than an assignment-specific plan library, and using a quantitative Top-*K*/EXAM evaluation protocol comparable to current LLM-FL and MBFL literature.

### Connection to AlgoRythm

This study originates from and is intended to inform AlgoRythm, an educational platform that pairs an algorithmic-pattern classifier with a novice-facing coding sandbox. If pattern-specific structural priors measurably improve automated fault localization, that result would motivate a pattern-conditioned debugging assistant inside AlgoRythm's tutor. No claim about AlgoRythm's effectiveness, its classifier's accuracy, its curriculum, or any student-learning outcome is made or supported by this pilot; the platform was not evaluated, no human subjects participated, and any future study that would speak to a human-learning outcome requires institutional review board or equivalent pre-approval obtained *before* data collection, independent of this pilot.

### The Research Gap

Two literatures that are each individually mature have not been connected. Algorithm-pattern recognition from source code is a largely solved classification problem: Watanobe et al. (2023) report F1 ≈ 95.7 classifying six to seven algorithm categories from structural, identifier-free features alone, and Neumüller et al.'s (2024) AlDeSCo system recognizes algorithmic motifs via hand-written AST patterns with an F1 more than double a general-purpose code LLM's on the same recognition task. Every one of these systems, and the adjacent COFO benchmark's "Infer the Technique" task, stops at recognition: none couples the recognized pattern to a downstream fault-localization step, and their own stated future work does not propose doing so. On the other side, the strongest current fault-localization systems for novice code — SBFL, MBFL, and LLM-based localizers alike (Yang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025) — are pattern-agnostic: none receives, as input, any classification of the algorithmic technique the program under test is attempting. The precise gap this study addresses is the untested causal link between these two mature literatures: whether handing a fault localizer the *classified algorithmic pattern*, in the form of a compact, pattern-specific failure checklist, measurably improves its Top-*K* localization accuracy over and above the improvement obtainable from *any* comparably formatted, comparably lengthed debugging guidance that is not pattern-specific. That "any guidance versus pattern-specific guidance" distinction — not merely "guidance versus no guidance" — is the gap, and it is also the reason this pilot's design centers on a three-arm, matched-placebo comparison rather than a simpler two-arm one (Method, "Experimental Conditions").

### Research Question and Hypotheses

**Research question.** Does supplying an algorithm-pattern-specific structural prior improve large-language-model-based fault localization in novice Python code, compared with a matched, pattern-agnostic ("generic") debugging prior of equal format, length, and instructional tone?

Framed as the causal chain the study is built to test: *algorithmic intent → structural prior → fault-localization ranking.* The manipulation is not "more guidance versus less guidance" but "guidance derived from the program's classified algorithmic pattern versus guidance that is not."

Because this is an explicitly exploratory, calibration-stage pilot at *n* = 30 (Method, "Statistical Analysis Plan"; and see the pre-declared power arithmetic in that section, which shows the design cannot reliably reach conventional statistical significance at this sample size), the hypotheses below are stated as the directional predictions the design is built to detect, not as claims the pilot is powered to confirm or refute:

- **H1 (primary, directional).** Programs will show a higher rate of correct Top-1 fault localization under the pattern-specific prior (condition C) than under the matched generic prior (condition B).
- **H0 (primary, the McNemar null).** Among programs on which the two conditions disagree, successes are distributed no differently from chance between "pattern-only" and "generic-only" outcomes.
- **Secondary, exploratory.** Both prior-bearing conditions (B and C) will show higher Top-1 accuracy than the no-prior condition (A), reflecting a general effect of receiving *any* delimited debugging checklist; this comparison cannot, by construction, attribute any such effect to pattern specificity (Method, "The Role of Condition A").
- **Calibration expectation.** The duplicated no-prior condition, run independently in each of the two experimental sessions (A_G and A_P), is expected to show near-zero discordance with itself at temperature 0; a non-trivial A_G–A_P discordance would indicate that provider-side drift between sessions is large enough to threaten interpretation of the primary comparison (Method, "Statistical Analysis Plan").

A null or statistically indecisive result on H1 must not be interpreted as evidence that pattern-specific priors have no effect: the sample size is far below what would be needed to rule that out (Method, "Statistical Analysis Plan").

## Related Work

### Automated Fault Localization: Spectrum- and Mutation-Based Methods

Spectrum-based fault localization (SBFL) ranks program elements by suspiciousness scores computed from test coverage: for element *e*, let *ef* and *ep* be the numbers of failing and passing tests that cover *e*, and *nf* and *np* the total numbers of failing and passing tests. Widely used formulas include Tarantula (Jones et al., 2002), $\text{Ochiai}(e) = ef / \sqrt{nf \times (ef + ep)}$, and DStar (Wong et al., 2013), $\text{DStar}(e) = ef^{\ast} / [ep + (nf - ef)]$ with the exponent typically set to 3. Wong et al.'s (2016) survey of over 150 SBFL papers remains the standard reference for the family. SBFL's central weakness for this study's population is documented directly: Araujo et al. (2016) report that a substantial fraction of novice programs violate SBFL's precondition of having at least one passing and one failing test, and short, structurally simple novice programs produce many suspiciousness ties, which SBFL resolves only by an arbitrary or conservative tie-breaking convention.

Mutation-based fault localization (MBFL) instead generates small syntactic mutants of each program element and observes whether mutating it flips failing tests to passing; a statement whose mutants "fix" more failures is scored more suspicious. Yang et al. (2025) is the direct precedent for this study's dataset and metrics: applying an MBFL protocol (Jaccard, Tarantula, Ochiai, OP2, and DStar formulas over mutant kill patterns) to 150 Python and 150 Java novice programs sampled from ConDefects, they report Python Top-1/Top-3/Top-5 counts of 45/70/92 out of 150 (best formula, OP2) and an average EXAM score of 0.284, at a cost of roughly 87 machine-hours for the Python subset alone — establishing both the metric protocol this study adopts (Method, "Measures") and the classical performance ceiling that motivates looking beyond spectrum- and mutation-based methods for novice code.

### Fault Localization and Debugging in Novice Programs

Beyond the SBFL/MBFL precondition problems above, a distinct line of education-focused fault-localization systems exploits *reference solutions* rather than program-pattern information. VsusFL (cited as a *Journal of Systems and Software* 2023 study of 422 faulty submissions) traces variable-value sequences at runtime, matches them to a single correct reference program for the same task via a bipartite-graph/Hungarian-algorithm alignment, and derives suspiciousness from the point of first divergence, substantially outperforming SBFL baselines on novice code. CLARA (cited as an ITiCSE 2018 study) clusters *many* correct student submissions by dynamic behavior and repairs an incorrect submission by aligning its execution trace to the nearest cluster. Both approaches use *a specific other program's* structure as the prior, not a *transferable, task-independent* pattern class — the distinction this study's design is built to test. Complementing the system-building literature, multi-institutional studies of how novices themselves debug (Fitzgerald et al., 2008; Lister et al., 2004) document weak, ad hoc strategies and a persistent gap between tracing ability and the ability to explain what code is for, reinforcing the motivation for automated tool support rather than relying on debugging skill alone.

### LLM-Based Debugging and Fault Localization

Xu et al. (2025) is the most directly relevant precedent for this study's method: across thirteen LLMs (including OpenAI's o3, o1-preview/mini, GPT-4/4o/3.5, DeepSeek-R1/V3, ChatGLM, and open Llama-family models) and three novice-fault datasets (Codeflaws in C, ConDefects in Java, and a leakage-controlled internal benchmark, "BugT," in C++), they establish the Top-1/Top-3/Top-5 and EXAM evaluation protocol, the practice of repeating each configuration five times and averaging to address LLM stochasticity, and the finding that reasoning-capable LLMs outperform SBFL and MBFL on almost every dataset while still degrading in accuracy as problem difficulty rises. Their prompt-component ablation — testing persona, self-explained intent, justification, chain-of-thought, and sorting instructions as five separable additions to the prompt — is a direct methodological template for the present study's own ablation of a single component (the debugging-prior block) while holding everything else in the prompt fixed. Critically, none of the thirteen models in Xu et al. (2025) receives any signal about the program's intended algorithmic pattern; MacNeil et al.'s (2024) comparison of GPT-3/GPT-4 against 964 students on logic-error detection is similarly pattern-agnostic. This study's manipulation — adding a delimited, pattern-conditioned or pattern-agnostic checklist block to an otherwise identical LLM prompt — sits directly inside the design space these two studies define but leaves untested.

### Program Intent and Algorithmic-Pattern Recognition

Algorithm-pattern recognition from source code is a mature classification task. Watanobe et al. (2023) classify accepted solutions from the Aizu Online Judge into six to seven algorithm categories using only structural, identifier-stripped features (loop/branch/operator tokens) fed to a shallow convolutional architecture, reporting precision/recall/F1 around 95–97%; a companion sorting-algorithm-only dataset reaches F1 ≈ 96.9%. Neumüller et al.'s (2024) AlDeSCo system recognizes algorithmic motifs (Fibonacci, bubble sort, binary search) via a hand-authored, domain-specific-language pattern-matching engine over abstract syntax trees, reporting F1 = 0.74 against 0.35 for a general-purpose code LLM baseline on the same recognition task. The COFO benchmark (cited in the project's literature review as a 2025 dataset of Codeforces solutions) defines an "Infer the Technique" task as a companion to "Infer the Task," explicitly separating algorithm-family identification from problem identification. All three lines of work stop at *recognition*: none conditions a downstream reasoning task — debugging, repair, or localization — on the recognized label, and their own discussions of future work do not propose doing so. This study takes the recognized-pattern *label* as an input (assigned here by human annotation under a frozen, blinded protocol rather than by a trained classifier; see Method, "Oracle Pattern Labels") and asks what a downstream localizer does with it — the coupling step the recognition literature has left untested.

### Structural and Contextual Priors in Automated Reasoning

Outside the education setting, industrial fault localization has begun to explore *functional-intent* priors: FaR-Loc (cited in the project's literature review as a September 2025 arXiv preprint) has an LLM generate a free-text description of a failing test's behavior and retrieves functionally similar covered methods via semantic embeddings, substantially improving Top-1/Top-5 over prior LLM-agent baselines on the industrial Defects4J benchmark. FaR-Loc's "intent" is an unstructured natural-language description of *this specific failure*, not a discrete, reusable class label describing the algorithm's *type*; it targets professional Java repositories, not novice code, and involves no pattern classifier. A separate line of work (cited in the project's literature review as a 2024 IEICE Transactions study) boosts SBFL by using *multiple* correct submissions to the same task to sharpen coverage spectra — again a per-task reference-program prior, not a transferable pattern class. None of these lines supplies the specific comparison this study is built around: a *matched* pattern-specific versus pattern-agnostic prior of identical format and length, isolating pattern content as the only systematic difference.

### Educational Programming Systems

Johnson's (1990) PROUST remains the closest conceptual ancestor across the entire literature reviewed for this study: a knowledge-based system that diagnosed novice Pascal bugs by comparing submitted code against a hand-authored library of expected programming plans for a specific assignment, explaining bugs as deviations from the intended plan. PROUST's plan library was authored per assignment, not classified from an open, transferable pattern vocabulary; it involved no learned classifier and no quantitative Top-*K* evaluation against localization baselines; and it predates every LLM- and mutation-based baseline this study compares against by roughly four decades. Ko and Myers's (2008) Whyline line of work is a related but distinct educational-debugging system: rather than supplying a structural prior, it lets the user ask "why did/didn't" questions about recorded program behavior and answers them via causal execution traces, reporting that Whyline users were markedly more successful and faster at real bug-finding tasks than with conventional tools. The Whyline answers *behavioral* questions about what a program did; the present study's pattern prior instead constrains *which* behavioral questions are structurally plausible to ask first, given the program's algorithmic type.

### Synthesis: The Gap This Study Addresses

Three literatures converge on the same untested joint: (a) algorithm-pattern recognition from source code is solved at high accuracy (Watanobe et al., 2023; Neumüller et al., 2024); (b) automated fault localization for novice code — classical (Yang et al., 2025) and LLM-based (Xu et al., 2025) alike — is pattern-agnostic and its accuracy is known to degrade on harder, more structurally involved faults; and (c) the intellectual precedent for using intended-plan knowledge in novice-fault diagnosis (Johnson, 1990) predates both the modern pattern-recognition and modern LLM-localization literatures and was never evaluated with a matched, quantitative ablation against a comparably-resourced generic-guidance control. This study supplies exactly that missing, matched ablation: a classified algorithmic pattern, expressed as a compact structural prior, compared against a text-, format-, and length-matched generic debugging prior, using the Top-*K*/EXAM protocol and paired-comparison statistics the adjacent literature already uses.

## Method

### Research Design Overview

This is a within-subjects, three-condition calibration pilot with a pre-registered, exploratory (non-confirmatory) statistical purpose. Thirty Python programs, each carrying exactly one human-verified single-line logical fault, are each scored under three prompt conditions that differ *only* in the debugging-guidance block appended to an otherwise identical prompt: **A** (`no_prior`, no block), **B** (`generic_prior`, a pattern-agnostic checklist matched in format and length to the pattern prior), and **C** (`pattern_prior`, a checklist specific to the program's algorithmic pattern class). Each (program, condition) cell is queried five times at temperature 0 with one frozen large language model. The engineering pipeline (built and operated independently of this manuscript's authorship) implements exactly two experimental conditions per run, so the three arms are realized as **two paired sessions** over the identical 30-program sample: **Session G** (generic), contrasting A_G against B, and **Session P** (pattern), contrasting A_P against C. The pre-registered **primary comparison is C versus B**, computed across the two sessions, not A versus anything — the rationale for that choice is central to the design and is given its own subsection below. All protocol, dataset, priors, and analysis-plan documents referenced in this section were frozen — committed to the project's version-control history — before any experimental result existed (Reproducibility).

### Dataset: ConDefects-Python

The data source is ConDefects, a fault-localization research corpus built from real Python submissions to AtCoder programming contests (contests ABC, ARC, and AGC), pairing each rejected ("buggy") submission with the same author's subsequently accepted ("fixed") submission and with official test data. ConDefects is distributed under CC BY-SA 4.0 with an MIT-licensed toolkit (repository `appmlk/ConDefects`), and the associated peer-reviewed empirical study (Yang et al., 2025) used the same corpus for a direct mutation-based fault-localization baseline on novice Python and Java code, which this study's Method and Measures sections deliberately track for comparability. Problem statements (`problem_context`) were omitted from every prompt in every condition of this pilot, because AtCoder statements frequently make the intended algorithmic technique explicit, which would leak pattern information into the no-prior and generic-prior arms and shrink the very contrast the study exists to measure; this is a documented, all-or-none design decision, not an oversight, and its cost — a less realistic deployment setting — is accepted for this pilot and flagged for revisit in a confirmatory study (Future Work).

### Ground-Truth Procedure

Fault location (`faulty_lines`) was determined under a frozen, ordered blinding protocol (`pilot/v2/GROUND_TRUTH_PROTOCOL.md`) designed specifically to prevent the treatment condition's content from shaping the very ground truth it is scored against. The pipeline requires that `faulty_lines` be derived from the buggy-to-fixed diff and the failing-test evidence alone, and *frozen* before the annotator engages in any pattern-level reasoning about the program; the annotator is required to have completed and recorded fault-location decisions for all candidates in a write-once file before opening any pattern-vocabulary or pattern-prior document. Fix shapes are adjudicated by a fixed decision table that includes single-line fixes (included), single-line fixes with cosmetic-only surrounding changes (included), and five categories of multi-line, multi-hunk, ambiguous, refactor, or non-algorithmic fixes (all excluded). This aggressive single-line filter is deliberate and its cost is disclosed: it biases the pilot toward simple, cleanly localized faults, which compresses the achievable spread between conditions and biases the design *against* detecting a pattern-versus-generic difference — the conservative direction for a design whose central risk is a false positive, not a false negative.

A second, mirror-image blinding requirement (`GROUND_TRUTH_PROTOCOL.md` §6) governs pattern labeling, which necessarily happens *after* the fault line is frozen and therefore known to the annotator: the labeler is restricted to the buggy source, task metadata, and the pattern-vocabulary class *definitions* only (which contain no failure-checkpoint lists), must not have the fault-location record open while labeling, and must exclude any program whose label choice could plausibly have been influenced by knowledge of the known fault line. With a single annotator — the case at this pilot's scale — this is disclosed as a discipline-and-exclusion control rather than a structural guarantee, because a person cannot fully unsee information they recorded earlier in the same pipeline.

### One-Program-per-Task Policy

At most one program per unique AtCoder `task_id` may enter the sampling frame, enforced at stage S4 by a seeded random draw (seed 20260910) among eligible same-task candidates. This constraint exists because same-task programs share a problem statement, an intended technique, and frequently a fault mode; the project's paired statistical tests (exact McNemar; program-clustered bootstrap) treat each included program as an independent unit, and enforcing task-level uniqueness at the sampling stage is what makes that independence assumption defensible, since no stage of the downstream analysis code separately checks or corrects for task-level clustering.

### Corrected v2.1 Sampling

An initial sampling pass (v2.0) applied the dynamic pass/fail-test precondition (Table M1, row S2c) *after* task-level deduplication and pattern-class selection rather than before, which allowed three programs with no passing tests on the buggy version to enter the frozen sample — a violation of the protocol's own eligibility rule, discovered during a subsequent audit rather than at the time of the original draw. The sample was corrected (v2.1) by re-running the complete candidate pool through the dynamic precondition *before* deduplication and class selection, as the frozen protocol specifies, which propagated through the entire downstream pipeline: the deduplicated frame shrank from 566 to 232 tasks, and the selected pattern classes changed (Session v2.0 drew from brute-force implementation, dynamic programming, graph traversal, and binary search; the corrected v2.1 frame instead supports brute-force implementation, dynamic programming, binary search, and sorting-based, because `graph_traversal_dfs_bfs` fell from 75 nominally eligible candidates to only 17 once the dynamic precondition was applied — roughly 77% of novice graph-traversal submissions in this corpus fail the precondition, chiefly via judge timeouts and recursion-limit errors). Twenty-eight of the 30 final programs differ between v2.0 and v2.1; two program IDs (45028964, 45966660) happened to survive the correction unchanged. The v2.1 sample is the one used throughout the remainder of this Method section and is the only sample this manuscript treats as frozen.

### Final Sample (n = 30)

### Algorithm-Pattern Taxonomy

The frozen pattern vocabulary (`pilot/PATTERN_VOCABULARY.md`) defines twelve mutually exclusive classes, each with a written definition and an explicit confusable-pair disambiguation rule: two pointers, sliding window, binary search, prefix sums, dynamic programming, a greedy strategy, graph or grid traversal (DFS or BFS, merged into one class for this vocabulary version), a sort-then-scan approach, hash-map or hash-set counting, direct brute-force implementation, interval merging or scheduling, and step-by-step simulation. The vocabulary is grounded in the classification-feasibility evidence reviewed in Related Work (Watanobe et al., 2023; Neumüller et al., 2024) and in AlgoRythm's existing AST-based sandbox classifier; it is frozen for both this pilot and any future confirmatory corpus, and any change to a class name or slug requires a new, separately versioned document.

### Selected Pattern Classes

Class selection follows a deterministic, outcome-independent rule fixed before any pattern counting occurred (`SAMPLING_PROTOCOL.md` §6): compute the eligible-program count per class in the deduplicated frame, rank classes by that count in descending order (ties broken by the pattern vocabulary's fixed table row order), and take the top four, allocating 8/8/7/7 in rank order. In the corrected v2.1 frame (|F| = 232), the ranked eligible counts were `brute_force_implementation` = 94, `dynamic_programming` = 45, `binary_search` = 22, `sorting_based` = 21, `graph_traversal_dfs_bfs` = 17, `outside_vocabulary` = 16 (not a selectable class), `greedy` = 7, `hash_map_counting` = 5, `simulation` = 3, and `prefix_sums` = 2; `two_pointers` and `intervals` recorded zero eligible candidates in this frame. The top four — brute-force implementation, dynamic programming, binary search, and sorting-based — were selected because they alone exceeded the ≥7-eligible-programs class-inclusion threshold by rank order under this rule; the selection procedure could not have been steered toward classes expected to perform well, because it uses only frame counts computed before any model was run. The consequence, disclosed as a scope limitation rather than absorbed silently, is that this pilot's discordance estimate describes these four classes only, not the full twelve-class vocabulary (Threats to Validity and Limitations).

### Oracle Pattern Labels

Under the frozen annotation protocol (`pilot/ANNOTATION_GUIDE.md` §2; `GROUND_TRUTH_PROTOCOL.md` §6), each program's `pattern_label` was to be assigned by a human annotator, working from the AtCoder task's reference/intended technique together with a structural read of the *candidate's own* buggy submission, applying the twelve-class vocabulary's definitions and confusable-pair rules in their stated order — blinded to the frozen fault location and to the pattern-specific failure checklists — with a `pattern_source` field recording a short, human-readable provenance string (e.g., `"atcoder-task-tag+manual-verification"`) for later audit.

**A discrepancy requiring resolution before any Results section is finalized.** The frozen manifest that this study treats as authoritative records `pattern_source: "AST rule"` — not a human-annotation provenance string of the form the annotation guide specifies — identically for all 30 included programs. This is inconsistent with the mandatory disclosure this project's own claim-boundary document requires whenever a pilot number is reported: that "pattern labels were assigned by a human annotator... not predicted by a model" (`pilot/v2/CLAIM_BOUNDARIES_V2.md` §4.1). This manuscript does not resolve the discrepancy by asserting either account. It is possible that `"AST rule"` is an artifact of how the dataset-engineering pipeline populated the `pattern_source` field programmatically after a human decision was made elsewhere and not re-recorded verbatim, or it is possible that a mechanical rule materially contributed to labeling in a way the annotation guide did not anticipate; the committed repository record does not, on its own, distinguish between these. Before any Results section of this paper is written, the project must reconcile this field — either by correcting the manifest's recorded provenance to accurately describe the process that was actually followed, or by revising the Method and Discussion sections and the mandatory disclosures to accurately describe an AST-rule contribution to labeling. Until reconciled, any claim in this or a future version of this paper that the pilot uses "oracle, human-assigned" pattern labels should be read as the *protocol's specification*, not as an independently verified fact about the frozen manifest as committed. This is logged as the single highest-priority open item in `paper/PAPER_SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

### Experimental Conditions

The three prompt conditions share one system prompt and one user-message template (Table M4 gives the frozen prompt scaffold), and differ only in whether, and what, a single delimited block (`<ALGORITHMIC_PATTERN_PRIOR>…</ALGORITHMIC_PATTERN_PRIOR>`) is inserted:

- **A — `no_prior`.** The shared system prompt and user template only. No prior block. Note that condition A is *not* a zero-guidance baseline: the shared system prompt already instructs the model to reason "like a careful code reviewer," look for divergence "at the boundary between the programmer's intended plan and the code that implements it," and specifically consider "loop bounds, comparison directions, index updates, initialization, and the order in which state is updated" — generic reviewer framing present identically in all three conditions. Condition A means "no prior *block*," not "no guidance at all," and this must be disclosed whenever a comparison involving A is reported.
- **B — `generic_prior`.** Condition A plus a delimited, four- or five-checkpoint pattern-agnostic debugging checklist (`pilot/v2/generic_placebo_prior.json`), covering loop-bound correctness, comparison-operator direction, accumulator initialization/update order, and boundary/edge-case handling — failure classes documented as recurring across novice logic errors independent of algorithm family (Ettles et al., 2018; and the related three-decade survey cited in the project's literature review as Alzahrani & Vahid, 2021).
- **C — `pattern_prior`.** Condition A plus a delimited checklist specific to the program's `pattern_label`, drawn from the frozen pattern-priors document (`pilot/pattern_priors.json`), naming the intended pattern by name and listing four or five pattern-specific failure checkpoints in a fixed order (e.g., for `dynamic_programming`: base-case value and index, transition completeness over all predecessor states, fill order versus recurrence dependency, memo/table initialization value, and index-offset consistency).

**Structural parity between B and C is mechanically audited, not asserted.** Across the twelve pattern-and-placebo text pairs, checkpoint count matches exactly for every label; word-count deviation is at most 8 words (mean 4.2) against prior lengths of 119–138 words; the opening-sentence clause structure, the closing hedge sentence ("These are hypotheses, not conclusions…"), and the per-checkpoint "check whether…" phrasing are identical in form across every pair; and a mechanical scan confirms the placebo text contains none of the pattern-vocabulary's own terminology (no mention of pointers, windows, midpoints, recurrences, memos, traversal, hashing, or the other twenty-plus flagged terms). One documented, disclosed asymmetry survives: the delimiter tag `<ALGORITHMIC_PATTERN_PRIOR>` wraps genuinely pattern-specific content in condition C but wraps generic content of the same name in condition B, which is the one directional bias in this design that favors detecting an effect rather than working against it (see "Threats to Validity and Limitations"). Because a competent generic checklist necessarily overlaps with the pattern-agnostic *parts* of each pattern-specific checklist, the design's authors note that any measured C − B effect should be read as a conservative lower bound on the value of pattern-specific information, and that the surviving alternative explanation — that per-program *tailoring on any axis*, not specifically pattern-based tailoring, drives an observed effect — is not ruled out by this three-arm design and would require a fourth, non-pattern-tailored arm to exclude.

### Why C vs. B Is the Primary Comparison

This design choice follows directly from a documented failure of the project's first-generation (v1) specification. The v1 design compared only a no-prior condition against the pattern-specific prior; an internal adversarial pre-run review of that design concluded it could not attribute any observed effect to pattern specificity, because moving from no-prior to pattern-prior simultaneously changes at least five things: the pattern-specific content itself, roughly 120–140 additional words of prompt, the presence of a numbered checklist format, an explicit "check these first, in order" attention directive, and the meta-signal that the program has a recognizable algorithmic structure at all. A positive v1-style result would have been fully explained by the alternative hypothesis "any structured debugging guidance helps," independent of whether that guidance mentions the correct pattern. The generic-prior condition B is constructed specifically to hold every one of those confounds constant — same delimiter, same numbered-checklist format, same hedged instructional tone, matched checkpoint count, and word length matched within the declared ±10-word tolerance — while varying only whether the checklist's *content* is derived from the program's classified pattern. The comparison **C vs. B** is therefore the only comparison in this design that isolates the causal contribution of pattern-specific structural information; the two secondary comparisons, A vs. B and A vs. C, each measure the *total* effect of receiving some prior at all (relative to a condition that is itself not zero-guidance) and cannot, on their own, be attributed to pattern specificity under any circumstance, regardless of their outcome.

### The Role of Condition A

Condition A serves the design in two ways that do not depend on it being interpretable as a zero-guidance floor. First, because the engineering pipeline realizes the three arms as two sessions that must each contain a control condition, arm A is necessarily executed twice, independently, on the identical 30-program sample — once in Session G (A_G) and once in Session P (A_P). This duplication is a byproduct of the tooling's two-condition-per-session design, not an intentionally engineered feature, but it is put to genuine use as a **null calibration**: because A_G and A_P are two independent replicates of the literally identical condition, any discordance between them is attributable to nothing but re-running (temperature-0 non-determinism, provider-side drift between the two sessions, or both), and this discordance count is used as a pre-declared gate on the interpretability of the primary comparison (Statistical Analysis Plan, below). Second, A vs. B and A vs. C, while not able to attribute an effect to pattern specificity, remain reportable as secondary, fully-qualified measures of the total effect of providing any structured debugging checklist relative to the (non-zero-guidance) shared system prompt alone.

### Large Language Model Implementation

The scientific hypothesis and its experimental design (above) are deliberately described independently of any specific model, because the model configuration is an engineering/transport-layer decision subject to pre-results technical amendment, whereas the design is frozen. As currently configured, every condition is executed with a single, pinned large language model, **z-ai/glm-5.3-flash**, accessed through the OpenRouter API (`https://openrouter.ai/api/v1`) with the underlying inference provider explicitly pinned to **Z.AI** (routing endpoint `z-ai/fp8`, provider fallbacks disabled, `require_parameters` enabled), at **temperature 0**, with **five repetitions** per (program, condition) cell. Every call's echoed model and provider identity is verified against the requested identity on every response; any mismatch is treated as an immediate, run-invalidating failure under the project's frozen model-freeze protocol. Under temperature 0, the five repetitions are interpreted not as an averaging device for stochastic output (the precedent this repetition count is drawn from, Xu et al., 2025, uses non-zero-temperature models) but as an explicit **determinism probe**: the fraction of (program, condition) cells whose five raw responses are byte-identical, and separately the fraction whose five Top-1 outcomes agree, is a mandatory reported diagnostic, because temperature 0 is not a guarantee of determinism under batched or mixture-of-experts serving, and if determinism turns out to be high, the effective information content of the pilot is 30 paired observations per comparison, not 150.

**The completion-token allowance (`max_tokens`) is a technical execution parameter, not part of the frozen scientific design, and its value has changed twice already, before any scientific result existed, in direct response to observed truncation failures — and it may change again.** The parameter began at 1,024, was raised to 4,096 after a smoke-test response terminated at that limit before producing a parseable completion, and was raised again to 16,384 after the first full scientific execution attempt (Session G, `20260911T181032Z-G`) was declared VOID at its very first call, whose recorded cost was later diagnosed as an exact match for 4,096 completion tokens fully consumed, evidence-consistent with the model exhausting its entire output budget on internal reasoning tokens before emitting any visible answer content. A dedicated stress-test at the 16,384-token limit, run only against non-scientific synthetic fixtures, passed cleanly (20/20 calls, `finish_reason: stop`, non-null content). **The subsequent full scientific rerun under the 16,384-token limit also terminated in a VOID state**, this time seventeen calls in: its eighteenth call returned a valid HTTP response with the correctly echoed model and provider identity but `content = null`, `finish_reason = length`, and 16,384 output tokens of which 16,383 were internal reasoning tokens — the identical failure signature observed at the earlier, lower limit, now recurring at four times the allowance. This is reported here explicitly so that **16,384 must not be read, anywhere in this manuscript or in any future version of it, as the final, successfully validated completion-token configuration** — as of this writing it is demonstrably insufficient for at least one real program in this pilot's own sample, and a further pre-results technical amendment (raising the limit again, and/or constraining the provider's internal reasoning-token budget if the API exposes a mechanism to do so) is required before the next execution attempt. Every one of these amendments occurred strictly before any scientific model output was inspected for localization performance, was authorized on mechanical validity grounds alone (parseable, non-truncated, non-null responses on held-out smoke or stress fixtures — never candidate programs from the frozen sample), and is recorded with a timestamp, a diagnosed cause, and a validation smoke-test result in the project's version-control history; none altered the dataset, the priors, the prompts, the sampling, or the statistical analysis plan. ## Measures

### Top-*K* Accuracy

For program *i*, condition *X*, and repetition *r*, let the model's response be a ranked list of suspicious line numbers. **Top-*K*** scores 1 if any of the program's ground-truth faulty line(s) appears within the first *K* entries of that ranking, and 0 otherwise. Ties are resolved conservatively: a line tied with *k* others at the same suspicion score is assigned the *worst* (last) position within that tied block, following the convention Yang et al. (2025) justify by noting that a majority of developers inspect only the top five suggestions in practice. This pilot reports **Top-1** (the pre-registered primary endpoint), **Top-3**, and **Top-5** (both secondary). Because every included program carries exactly one ground-truth faulty line under this pilot's single-line-fault filter, the "best-rank-across-multiple-faulty-lines" convention used by the project's scoring code for multi-line faults is inactive for this pilot's sample and does not affect interpretation here.

### EXAM and EXAM\*

The EXAM score for a single program and response is the proportion of the file a developer would need to inspect, in ranked order, before reaching the first true faulty line:

$$\text{EXAM}(i) = \frac{\text{rank of the first faulty line in the returned ranking}}{D}$$

where *D* is the evaluation denominator. If no faulty line appears anywhere in the returned ranking, EXAM is censored at its maximum: rank is set to *D*, so EXAM = 1.0. Lower EXAM values indicate better localization. This pilot's denominator *D* is the buggy file's **physical line count**, applied uniformly to all 30 programs, rather than the count of semantically inspectable statements that the original EXAM metric (as defined and reported in Yang et al., 2025) uses. This project therefore reports **EXAM\***, marked with an asterisk deliberately, to flag that it is a **censored, line-based** statistic that is **not numerically comparable to published EXAM values**, including those in Yang et al. (2025), and it must never be presented as if it were.

## Statistical Analysis Plan

### Experimental Unit and Aggregation

**The primary experimental unit of this study is one program, not one LLM invocation.** The five repetitions per (program, condition) cell are repeated measurements of the same unit under the same condition, not five independent samples; pooling 30 programs × 5 repetitions into an *n* = 150 analysis is explicitly prohibited by the pre-registered plan and is not performed anywhere in this manuscript. For each program *i* and condition *X*, a single binary Top-1 outcome is derived by a pre-declared majority rule fixed before any data existed: $H_X^1(i) = 1$ if at least 3 of the 5 repetitions hit Top-1, and 0 otherwise (equivalent to thresholding the mean hit rate at 0.5; no tie is possible with an odd repetition count). The analogous EXAM\* quantity per program and condition is simply the mean of the five repetitions' EXAM\* values. If the determinism check described above shows all five repetitions agreeing on every program, this majority rule becomes a no-op, and that fact will itself be reported rather than silently absorbed.

### Primary Analysis

**Primary comparison: C vs. B, Top-1, at the program level, across the two sessions.** For the 30 paired binary outcomes $\{(H_C^1(i), H_B^1(i))\}$, let $b$ = the count of programs where C hits and B misses ("pattern-only" successes) and $c$ = the count where B hits and C misses ("generic-only" successes); $d = b + c$ is the number of discordant pairs. The pre-registered primary test is the **exact McNemar test**: a two-sided exact binomial test of $\min(b, c) \sim \text{Binomial}(d, 0.5)$. Because this comparison spans two separately executed sessions, it can only be computed after a set of cross-session invariants is asserted to hold exactly — identical dataset-manifest hash, identical system-prompt and user-template hashes, identical per-program shared-prompt hash, identical provider/model/temperature/`max_tokens`, identical repetition count and condition-order policy, and non-mock execution status — on *both* sessions; any violation is treated as invalidating the primary comparison outright rather than something to statistically adjust for.

### Null Calibration

**Before the primary result is interpreted, a pre-declared calibration gate must be applied.** Let $d_{\text{null}}$ be the discordant-pair count between the two independent replicates of condition A (A_G vs. A_P, Top-1, same 30 programs), an empirical noise floor attributable to nothing but re-running the identical condition across two sessions. If $d_{\text{null}} \geq d_{\text{primary}}$ (the discordant-pair count for C vs. B), **the primary comparison is declared uninterpretable at this sample size and must be reported as such regardless of its p-value.** This rule was fixed before either quantity could be known and may not be revised after the fact.

### Secondary and Robustness Analyses

Secondary analyses, all reported without alpha adjustment and explicitly labeled as secondary or descriptive, include: **A vs. C** (within Session P) and **A vs. B** (within Session G), each carrying the mandatory disclosure that condition A is not a zero-guidance baseline; a **program-clustered percentile bootstrap** (10,000 iterations, fixed seed 20260908) on the mean Top-1 difference $H_C - H_B$ and on the mean EXAM\* difference $E_C - E_B$; a **paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test** on the per-program EXAM\* differences $E_C(i) - E_B(i)$; a **descriptive, non-inferential** per-pattern-class breakdown of the Top-1 C − B delta within each of the four sampled classes (7–8 programs each — explicitly too small to support a per-class hypothesis test); and, as a **robustness check reported in full or not at all**, the five per-repetition exact McNemar tests that the underlying analysis code computes natively per session, none of which may be selectively quoted in isolation.

### Multiplicity and Power

Exactly **one** endpoint (Top-1, C vs. B) is designated primary and pre-registered; every other reported quantity is secondary or descriptive by design, and the protection against selective reporting is that this full list was fixed in advance and every item on it must be reported whether or not it is favorable to the hypothesis, rather than any formal multiplicity correction. The arithmetic of exact McNemar at *n* = 30 bounds what this pilot's p-value can mean and is reported here in full rather than only alluded to: the minimum attainable two-sided p-value as a function of the discordant count $d = b + c$, in the best case where every discordant pair favors the same condition, is 1.000 at $d = 1$; 0.500 at $d = 2$; 0.250 at $d = 3$; 0.125 at $d = 4$; 0.0625 at $d = 5$; **0.031 at $d = 6$**; 0.016 at $d = 7$; and 0.008 at $d = 8$. **At least six discordant pairs, all favoring the same condition, are required for $p \leq .05$ to be reachable at all**, and nine are required if even a single discordant pair runs the other way. A null or statistically indecisive result at this sample size is therefore uninformative about the absence of an effect and must not be reported as such. The pilot's actual statistical purpose, beyond a directional read of H1, is to estimate the discordance rate $\pi_d = d/30$ and the direction ratio $\psi = b/(b+c)$ well enough to size a future confirmatory study via the standard McNemar sizing approximation, reported as a bootstrap-derived range rather than a point estimate, because both quantities are themselves estimated from only 30 pairs.

## Discussion

Because no valid result exists, this section contains only the parts of the Discussion that do not depend on which way the data turn out, plus four labeled, mutually exclusive interpretation placeholders — one of which will be substantively expanded once a valid result is available, with the other three either removed or retained briefly for contrast, following the reporting order fixed in the Statistical Analysis Plan.

Whatever the eventual result, three framing points hold unconditionally, because they follow from the design rather than from the data. First, only the C-vs-B comparison can speak to pattern specificity; any effect observed only in A-vs-B or A-vs-C comparisons measures the effect of receiving *some* structured checklist, not of that checklist being pattern-specific, and must not be reported using language that implies otherwise. Second, this is a calibration pilot, not a confirmatory study, and no result at *n* = 30 — positive, null, or negative — licenses a claim that the underlying hypothesis has been confirmed or refuted; the design's actual statistical job is to produce a discordance-rate estimate that sizes a future, adequately powered study. Third, every quantitative claim drawn from this pilot must carry, in the same passage, the five mandatory disclosures fixed in advance: that oracle-labeling status is pending reconciliation with the "AST rule" discrepancy documented in Method (and, until reconciled, should not be asserted as fully human-oracle-labeled); that the sample is small (30 programs, and effectively 30 paired observations per comparison rather than 150, if the determinism rate turns out to be high); that the study is exploratory; that the sample is filtered toward the easiest class of faults; and that problem-context text was withheld from the model in every condition.

### Interpretation Scenario A: Positive Primary Effect

If the null-calibration gate passes ($d_{\text{null}} < d_{\text{primary}}$) and the exact McNemar test on Top-1 favors condition C, the maximal permitted claim — stated with every mandatory qualifier in the same passage, per the project's frozen claim-boundary document — is that, on this calibration sample of 30 ConDefects-Python programs with single-line faults across four algorithmic-pattern classes and (pending the label-provenance reconciliation above) oracle pattern labels, using one named model at temperature 0, providing an algorithm-pattern-specific structural prior improved fault localization relative to matched generic debugging guidance. This would not license any claim of pattern-specificity based on A-vs-C alone, any generalization beyond ConDefects-Python, the four sampled classes, single-line faults, or the one evaluated model, any claim about classifier effectiveness (no classifier was in the loop; labels were human-assigned), or any claim about AlgoRythm, student learning, or educational outcomes.

### Interpretation Scenario B: Null Effect

A statistically indecisive Top-1 result is, in fact, the outcome the design's own authors consider *most likely* at this sample size, given a deliberately strong placebo, a fault-filtering procedure that admits only the easiest, most cleanly localized bugs, and the arithmetic constraint that at least six one-directional discordant pairs are required before conventional significance is even reachable (Statistical Analysis Plan, "Multiplicity and Power"). A null result under this design must be reported as **uninformative about the absence of a pattern-specificity effect**, not as evidence against the hypothesis, and the pilot's value in this scenario would lie entirely in the discordance-rate and direction-ratio estimates it nonetheless produces for sizing a properly powered confirmatory study — including the possibility, itself a legitimate and pre-named outcome rather than a failure, that the observed direction ratio lands close enough to 0.5 that the sizing formula cannot produce a usable estimate at all.

### Interpretation Scenario C: Negative Effect

If the exact McNemar test favors condition B over condition C, this manuscript will report that finding without qualification or suppression, alongside the same mandatory disclosures as Scenario A, and alongside careful consideration of the one documented bias in this design that runs in the *opposite* direction from a negative finding: the `<ALGORITHMIC_PATTERN_PRIOR>` delimiter tag wraps genuinely pattern-specific content in condition C but wraps generic content of the same name in condition B, a mismatch a sufficiently capable model might notice and partially discount, which — if anything — should inflate C relative to B rather than depress it. A negative result obtained despite that pro-hypothesis bias would be a comparatively strong finding; a negative result would also warrant the manual spot-check the frozen protocol requires, examining raw condition-B transcripts for any sign that the model is discounting the mismatched delimiter tag or, conversely, deriving unintended benefit from it.

### Interpretation Scenario D: Mixed or Pattern-Dependent Effect

Given that this design deliberately concentrates the sample into four heterogeneous classes rather than spreading it thinly across twelve, a plausible outcome is that the primary pooled Top-1 comparison is indecisive while the descriptive per-class breakdown (Table 3) shows a directionally consistent gap in one or two classes — for instance, dynamic programming, whose priors are grounded in an independently supported empirical finding that even experienced programmers' recursion difficulties concentrate on base-case recognition (Baron & Feitelson, 2024). Any such pattern must be reported strictly as descriptive, at 7–8 programs per class, with no per-class inferential test, per the pre-registered analysis plan — and explicitly flagged as a hypothesis for a future, adequately powered study rather than as a finding this pilot can support on its own.

## Threats to Validity and Limitations

**Sample size and statistical power.** At *n* = 30 programs, the exact McNemar test cannot reach conventional significance unless at least six discordant pairs favor one condition; the pilot is explicitly not powered to confirm or refute the research hypothesis, and its primary statistical function is calibration for a future study, not a stand-alone test of the effect.

**Per-pattern sample sizes.** Each of the four sampled pattern classes contains only 7 or 8 programs, which supports descriptive reporting only; no per-class comparison in this study should be read as an inferential claim about that specific pattern.

**Single programming language.** All 30 programs are Python; nothing in this design speaks to Java, C++, or any other language, and the ConDefects corpus itself documents markedly different MBFL performance between its Python and Java subsets (Yang et al., 2025), so cross-language transfer of any result here cannot be assumed.

**Representativeness of ConDefects and its testcase archive.** ConDefects draws exclusively from AtCoder competitive-programming submissions, which differ systematically from classroom assignments, open-ended projects, or professional code in both style and fault distribution. Separately, the project's own Gate 7 audit of this pilot's test-case provenance concludes that the packaged input/output pairs originate from a **reproducible third-party mirror archive** (`conlacda/atcoder-testcases`), not a confirmed official AtCoder or ConDefects-maintained source, and that only 3–8 cases per task (211 total) were packaged, favoring small, example-style inputs; this is disclosed as a genuine limitation on how exhaustively each program's "fixed passes all tests" claim can be trusted, not merely a formality.

**Oracle pattern-label provenance discrepancy.** As detailed in Method, the frozen manifest records `pattern_source: "AST rule"` for all 30 programs, which is inconsistent with the blinded human-annotation provenance the frozen protocol specifies and that this project's own claim-boundary document requires disclosing. This is the single highest-priority unresolved item flagged by this review and must be reconciled before any Results section is finalized (see `paper/PAPER_SOURCE_AUDIT.md`).

**Single-annotator ground truth and labeling.** Both fault-location and pattern-labeling decisions were made by one annotator, with no second-rater agreement (κ) computed at this pilot's scale — an explicit, documented relaxation permitted for calibration purposes only, and mandatory to correct (double annotation, κ ≥ .70) before any confirmatory corpus.

**The single-line-fault filter biases the achievable effect size downward, not upward.** Restricting the sample to unambiguous, single-line, cleanly diff-localized faults removes the harder multi-line, multi-hunk, and refactor-shaped bugs that plausibly show the largest pattern-specific structure, which compresses the spread available between conditions in the conservative direction — but also means this pilot's discordance estimate will not transfer directly to a confirmatory corpus that admits harder faults.

**`problem_context` omission.** No program's prompt, in any condition, includes the AtCoder problem statement; this avoids leaking pattern information into the control arms but is a deliberately unrealistic simplification relative to how a debugging assistant would actually be deployed, and is flagged for revisit rather than treated as free of cost.

**Model-, provider-, and configuration-specificity.** Every result this pilot can produce is specific to one named model (`z-ai/glm-5.3-flash`) accessed through one pinned provider route at one fixed temperature; no claim of the form "large language models benefit from pattern priors" is supported by a single-model design, and the completion-token allowance remains an unresolved, actively-changing technical parameter as of this writing (Method, "Large Language Model Implementation"), which is itself evidence that this model's behavior under this task is not yet fully characterized.

**No classical or spectrum-/mutation-based baseline is run within this pilot.** SBFL and MBFL comparisons are cited from prior published work (Yang et al., 2025) as context, not re-executed on this exact 30-program sample; a same-sample classical baseline is deferred to future work.

**No human-subject or educational-outcome validation.** No student, teacher, or human debugger participated in this pilot in any capacity, and no claim about human debugging speed, comprehension, or learning outcome is supported by, or attempted by, this study.

**Possible benchmark contamination.** ConDefects is a public dataset assembled from public AtCoder submissions; the extent to which the evaluated model's training data overlaps with this specific corpus has not been probed in this pilot (a cheap partial probe — asking the model to reproduce a held-out task's canonical solution on non-manifest programs — is deferred to Future Work), and any eventual result should be read with that unprobed possibility in mind.

**Pattern-taxonomy limitations.** The twelve-class vocabulary merges DFS and BFS into a single class for lack of annotator-agreement data to support splitting them, and explicitly excludes any program whose intended technique falls outside the twelve classes (e.g., union-find, segment trees, pure formula derivation); this pilot's four sampled classes are a further, disclosed narrowing of that vocabulary, chosen by a deterministic, outcome-blind rule rather than by researcher judgment, but narrowing nonetheless.

**One documented pro-hypothesis asymmetry.** The shared delimiter tag names "algorithmic pattern" truthfully in condition C and misleadingly in condition B; every other identified bias in this design runs against detecting an effect, which makes this the one asymmetry warranting the closest scrutiny of any eventual positive result (Discussion, Scenario A and C).

---

## Future Work

A properly powered **confirmatory study**, sized from this pilot's observed discordance rate via the pre-registered McNemar sizing approximation (Statistical Analysis Plan, "Multiplicity and Power"), spanning the full twelve-class pattern vocabulary rather than four classes, with double annotation reaching κ ≥ .70, is the direct next step this pilot exists to enable. That confirmatory design should additionally include: a **predicted-label condition**, in which pattern labels come from a trained classifier (e.g., the structural-feature or AST-based classifiers reviewed in Related Work) rather than from a human annotator, to measure how much of any oracle-label effect survives realistic, error-prone automatic labeling; evaluation across **multiple large language models** spanning different providers, sizes, and reasoning-effort configurations, since this pilot's design is explicitly single-model and cannot support any claim about LLMs in general; **classical SBFL and MBFL baselines run on the identical sample**, rather than cited from prior published work on a different sample, to give the primary comparison a same-sample classical anchor; extension to **additional programming languages**, given ConDefects' own documented Python/Java performance gap; evaluation on **additional datasets** beyond ConDefects (e.g., Codeflaws, or a leakage-controlled internal benchmark analogous to Xu et al.'s 2025 "BugT"), to address representativeness and contamination concerns; a **human debugging study**, comparing novice debugging speed and accuracy with and without access to pattern-specific guidance, conducted only under the institutional pre-approval this project's own governing rules require before any human-subjects data collection begins; and, ultimately, an **educational evaluation embedded in AlgoRythm itself**, testing whether a pattern-conditioned debugging assistant built on a confirmed localization effect actually improves student debugging outcomes in deployment — a question this pilot does not and cannot address on its own.

---

## Conclusion

This report has not established, and does not claim to establish, whether algorithm-pattern-specific structural priors improve large language model fault localization relative to matched generic debugging guidance. What it has established is a fully specified, seeded, blinded, and version-controlled protocol capable of answering that question with a precisely bounded, pre-declared claim once a valid execution completes — a three-arm design whose primary comparison is constructed specifically to isolate pattern-specificity from the confound of generic structured guidance, a dataset and ground-truth pipeline audited stage by stage, a statistical analysis plan fixed before any data existed, and a reporting discipline that preserves every failed execution attempt as part of the record rather than treating it as noise to be discarded. The connection this study is built to test — algorithmic intent, expressed as a classified structural prior, shaping a fault-localization ranking — sits at the untested joint between two literatures that are each independently mature: algorithm-pattern recognition from source code, and automated fault localization for novice programs. Closing that gap responsibly requires reporting what the data show, not what the design was hoped to show, in both directions. *[FINAL EMPIRICAL CONCLUSION TO BE INSERTED AFTER VALID PILOT.]*
